Chapter 16: Coming apart

"Owie…"

Ollie's seated on the edge of the bunk, arm raised, paw splayed open. The gash runs diagonally across the inside of his wrist. Whatever caught him during the impact cut clean, but deep enough to part the fur and expose the pink-yellow tissue beneath.

I kneel on my good leg, the splinted one angled out behind me, and press the last makeshift butterfly of duct tape against the wound's edge. The tape bites into the fur and holds. He winces. I check the others—four in total, spaced evenly along the cut, pulling the edges together. They hold.

The bedsheet I tore earlier sits in a coiled strip across my knee. I take it and begin wrapping, winding the fabric around his wrist in slow, overlapping passes. Yellow soaks through from beneath, faint but visible. Red emergency light bleeds in from the cockpit, enveloping the edges of the fold-out table, the storage bench, the grass in its noodle cup on the workbench, Ollie's glossy fur. At least the alarms have stopped screaming.

Ollie's tail whips against the mattress. "I said owie."

*"*I heard you the first time."

"And you ignored me."

"It's not that bad."

"It is." He sniffs loudly. "I'm gonna need soooo much fent."

"You're not getting fent for a cut—"

"A deep cut!"

"For a deep cut on your arm." I pull the bandage tight and tuck the trailing end beneath itself. "Come back when you have a crushed ankle."

He lowers his arm and inspects the work, turning his wrist left and right. He then looks at me. His eyes go wide. Wider than usual. His ear-things droop, and his lower lip pushes forward just enough to reshape his entire muzzle.

Puppy-dog eyes. Or whatever the Expie equivalent is, though dogs don't usually beg you for fent.

I ignore his eyes and pull his sleeve down over the bandage. The once-grey fabric is damp and yellowed, stiff where the blood has soaked through and started to dry.

The bloodstain won't get any prettier. "Arms up. Hoodie's getting washed."

His tail swishes and his arms shoot up, paws reaching for the overhead constellations. Almost eager.

"Not so fast," I say, "or you'll open your wound."

"Do you know what would fix that?"

"Fent would *not *fix an open wound."

I crouch lower, gritting my teeth against the ankle, and slide my hands underneath the hem of the hoodie. My fingers push into warm fur, finding his waist.

And stop.

His tummy is so slim. The fur is a facade—a roundness that suggests health. A plush lie. I can feel the truth. There's a void under the fur. When I press in, the edges of his hip bones jut against my fingertips. I don't need to reach far to find his ribs.

He eats everything I put in front of him, and still he's so small.

Ollie's fur bristles beneath my palms. "T-that tickles…" His paws are still up, fingers trembling toward the ceiling.

But there's something else under his fur. My thumb grazes it as I shift my grip—a small indent in the fur, centred below his ribs. Hidden. I press into it without thinking, and the pad of my thumb finds a shallow dip in the skin beneath the fur.

It's more surprising than it has any right to be. I circle the indent with my thumb. Ollie's paws snap down to grip the edge of the bunk, and a tremor runs through his torso; he is ticklish indeed.

"You have a belly button?"

"Yeah?" He looks down at me, still twitching at my touch. "Why wouldn't I?"

I had pictured Expies being grown in giant glowing tubes of liquid somewhere in a laboratory basement. Not… born. Connected by a cord that had to be cut. Someone carried him. Gave birth to him.

Then someone gave him a number.

"Do you have one?" he asks.

"A belly button? Yeah."

"Prove it."

Guess it's anatomy hour. I smirk, shift my weight onto the crutch, and lift the hem of my shirt with my free hand. Just enough.

His head tilts. "That's what they look like?"

"Yeah."

"All of them?"

Every Expie he's ever known was covered in fur. He's never had a chance to see one bare. "Mine's a stock-standard belly button. Nothing special."

"It's weird!"

"Hey, I'm not the one with the glowing eyes."

He opens them extra-wide, and the orange flares. "But you like them, don't you?"

"No comment."

His ear-things shift, and the corners of his muzzle lift. He's filing my non-answer in the same mental drawer as every other deflection I've given him. The drawer is getting full.

What were we doing?

Right. The hoodie. Ollie reads my mind before I open my mouth; his arms shoot up again, paws clasping for the ceiling.

I grip the hem and pull upward. The fabric catches on his ear-things, and I ease it over, working the collar past the base of each until the hoodie slides free. I toss it across the cabin. It lands in the wire basket by the sink with a thwap, yellow-stained sleeve hanging over the rim.

Ollie sits on the bunk as naked as the day I met him. Which, truth be told, doesn't feel very naked. The fur hides so much—bones, scars, belly button. He does look smaller without the hoodie, though.

"If you get cold," I say, "you can go back to wearing my jacket."

"Don't you need them more than me? You know, because of your… lack of fur?"

"Kind of you to think of me," I say, "after you've been using them as your nest for the past week."

"Oh… sorry." He glances down, snout curling into something resembling a sheepish smile. "But if you ever get too cold, you can always borrow my fur."

I open my mouth, but my mind blanks at trying to picture how one borrows fur. Like, what, shear him? Treat his orange-tipped tail like a campfire and warm my hands over it?

He doesn't elaborate. He just looks down at his bandaged wrist, turns it, then looks up at me.

"Did you have to kill them?"

I flinch. As usual, the question comes out of nowhere. Ollie's got a knack for the emotional ambush.

The wreckage is still out there. Drifting apart, piece by piece, in the dark behind us. Three men inside it. Or what's left of them.

"They were bad people," I say.

"Do bad people deserve to die?"

The red light throbs faintly from the cockpit. Calyx's beating heart.

"What they tried to do to us, they would have done to someone else." My voice is steady. Too steady. "They can't hurt anyone now."

The words sounded better in my head. Out loud, in this dim cabin, the seams in the answer split the longer the silence holds. It's a different excuse to what I said in the moment.

Ollie can smell the bullshit. He's not stupid. He's never been stupid.

But he says nothing. His expression is just… empty. Waiting to feel something he hasn't decided on yet. His paws rest in his lap, one bandaged, one bare, both still. And I'm left standing over him on one good leg, waiting for a judgement that never comes.

The red light has stopped blinking.

Not because anything's fixed. Because Ollie spent fifteen minutes rattling through Calyx's subdeck and decided the wire responsible for the alarm deserved to die.

His justification, delivered upside-down through the floor grille with a cable between his teeth: "We already know about the damage. And red warning lights make me anxious."

Hard to argue. In all my years aboard the Calyx, I've never seen anything more alarming than a blinking yellow. And I'd rather not be reminded every three seconds that we're limping through deep space with half a ship.

The one mercy is that the engine nacelle took the brunt before the force could travel deeper into the hull. Calyx is intact. Even the crates in the hold seem to be in one piece. But the portside engine is dead. We won't know how dead until someone has a chance to look into its guts. That makes the Calyx a one-legged cripple now.

"If only I could inspect the engine in person. Then, maybe…"

Ollie may be a mechanical genius, but not even geniuses can mend the outside of a ship in a vacuum. He's beside the pilot's chair, skimming the engine status screens. His bandaged paw taps through page after page of alerts. I've never seen so many. They don't even fit on the display. The darker shades of red are alerts about missing engine parts—parts I didn't know existed until they stopped existing.

"Is there another platform like Harlow nearby?" he asks, still scrolling.

"In deep space out in the middle of nowhere? Best I can do is land on an asteroid."

I adjust the joystick for the dozenth time. Calyx limps at half speed on the starboard engine alone, and the asymmetry produces a consistent lean to port that needs manual correction every few minutes. Like walking with a crutch.

"Say, any chance Expies can breathe in space?" I ask.

His head tilts. "Um… I don't actually know. I've never tried it. I never got to try much of anything."

"That wasn't a serious question."

His eyes narrow—seems he gave that question more thought than it deserved. "Well, if you kept a hazard suit on hand—"

"In case you forgot, the Calyx is a courier, not a military cruiser."

"A courier with laser turrets."

"Then maybe you should've asked Myra for a hazard suit."

Ollie huffs. He silently retires to the cabin. He's done all he can out here.

All things considered, a hauler going up against a missile launcher and flying away with one dead engine is a miracle. We should be debris. But we're not. We're alive and mobile—still crawling our way toward Lindenfield on one stubborn engine.

Maybe Lindenfield will give us a chance to patch up whatever's left of the portside engine. We can beg or bluff our way into a repair bay while we figure out what the hell comes next.

"I'm going to turn down the heating," Ollie shouts from the cabin.

"My jackets aren't that warm, are they?"

"Well the engines generate most of the onboard power, and we're running on one engine, so…"

Great. Last thing we need right now is to blow a fuse, so it's the right call. But I may need to borrow Ollie's fur after all. If only I had some shears.

For now at least, the leftover adrenaline is keeping me warm. My shirt is still stuck to my chest, damp with sweat.

Lindenfield. We're crawling there out of some sense of obligation to deliver crates full of metal parts I no longer give a shit about. That was the job. The whole reason we pointed ourselves back at whatever Ollie's running from. I still remember hammering at my datapad, accepting those three contracts and feeling like the luckiest courier alive. Then killing someone with a brick.

That one was self-defence, at least.

"Do you think one of the crates will have a hazard suit?" Ollie asks from the cabin.

"We're lucky," I shout back, "but not that lucky."

So why are we still going to Lindenfield—the first place anyone would look for us?

Well, money's part of it. Can't keep running without money. Fuel and food costs money. Repairing a blown-up engine costs money.

But then what? Where do we run to? What would stop the company from chasing us to the ends of the known universe, broadcasting our description until someone with better aim finishes what those pirates couldn't?

"There's no hazard suit on the manifest," says Ollie, still communicating via shouts from the cabin. "But what if it's being smuggled with other cargo—like the fent?"

"A bulky but completely legal piece of equipment being smuggled in secret?"

"Yeah! I can start prying open all the crates if you want to check."

"Honestly, I think we'd have more luck teaching you to breathe in space." I pause. "Don't try either of those things, by the way."

All we've got waiting near Lindenfield is a wildcard. Myra's boss. A pirate captain who, according to Myra, once helped a certain Expie escape. But that wildcard's buried under a mountain of assumptions. Pirate bosses aren't known for doing a good deed out of the kindness of their heart. And I don't know why I think some drunken 'ne'er-do-well' can help me outsmart the company for long enough to figure out a permanent solution—if such a solution even exists.

God, I miss the fent. It wasn't just the ankle it was sedating. It was this. The spiral. Pain and pessimism breaking me down.

I could ask Ollie for a top-up. He'd do it without hesitation. He'd probably enjoy the ritual of it, peeling the cap, finding the vein. It'd numb the thoughts. Stop me from reckoning with the fact I murdered three men and don't know why.

But I need to be lucid. Tomorrow, if Kathy's timeline holds, Solen calls. Tomorrow, I'll see that prick's charcoal suit and oil-slick hair for the first time in what feels like ages. I can barely remember his face. Just that smile that never fades.

"What are you going to talk to Kathy's boss about?" comes a question from the cabin.

He has impeccable timing. "Can you read my mind?"

"Huh? I don't think so."

Honestly, I don't know why I'd answer Solen's call. Knowing what they put Ollie through, the thought of looking at that smiling face and pretending everything's normal makes me sick. I'm going to have to endure that. Smile back. Solen plays the innocent corporate who hasn't put a bounty on my head, and I play the oblivious courier who doesn't know what's in his cargo.

And for what? What's Solen going to tell me that I haven't figured out already?

Well, there might be one thing, actually.

"Maybe I'll ask him about the Spindle."

I say it without thinking. Ollie's somewhere on the other side of the wall, his body language hidden. But the pause before his response is way too long.

"What's that?"

You can't play dumb, Ollie. I heard it from you. And one lie deserves another. "I heard you mumbling it in your sleep."

"That's… weird."

His words carry through the wall, too loud and careful for someone who doesn't know what it means. He's projecting uncertainty. But why?

There's also a soft but frantic whapping—his tail bouncing off the metal tiles. "So, maybe…" he says, "don't ask him about it?"

I suppose I shouldn't. I already know too much for the company's liking. For now, I'll trust that the seed he planted in Kathy's mind had a purpose.

But that leaves me on my own against Solen. I just need to treat it like a contract negotiation. One where I hold all the cards. They want Ollie back—alive. And the person most capable of delivering him? It isn't a sector's worth of trigger-happy pirates.

It's me. The terrifying unknown for the company… is me.

Come tomorrow, can I talk my way to something that doesn't end with Ollie in a cell and me in a ditch?

I need to try. For Ollie's sake. Solen is a man who can be bargained with. I just need to find the price where his loyalty to the company becomes flexible. Worst case, he extends the bounty. Short of us being dead, it's not like things could get much worse.

Behind me in the cabin, I hear the creak of the bunk. The rustle of jackets being rearranged. A small body settling into a nest that smells like me.

I lean back in the pilot's chair and correct the drift again. And again. And again.
