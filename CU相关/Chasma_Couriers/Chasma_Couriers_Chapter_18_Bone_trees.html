Chapter 18: Bone trees

We're going to save him.

The bunk is still warm where he was. I lie there, staring at the dead constellations on the ceiling, as my brain tries to make sense of the sentence—tries to give it a meaning that isn't a death sentence.

No such luck. I grab the crutch.

The cockpit is dim. Emergency lighting only. Ollie's in the pilot's chair, bare-furred and small against the seatback. His posture is different. Spine straight, paws on the armrests. Fur ruffled, bearing the marks my hands made only minutes ago.

His warmth has already bled out of my skin.

"Are you out of your tiny furry mind?"

His ear-things twitch. And that's all I get. His eyes stay on the viewport. Beyond the glass: nothing. Blacker than his fur.

"Don't ignore me." I plant the crutch behind the chair. "You somehow escaped from a prison that electrocuted you whenever you made a mistake—and now you want to waltz right back in?"

"I don't want to go back." His voice is rehearsed, almost like he's been practising while I lay in the bunk processing the shock. "I want to save my friend."

"Just because you word it differently doesn't change it. You'd just be throwing away everything I've done to try and keep you free." My hand finds the armrest and grips it. "And for what? Because you feel guilty?"

"You think this is freedom?"

He spins the pilot's chair and the swivel squeals. I hop backwards on one leg, keeping my broken ankle high above the ground.

"I'm stuck on this ship being hunted while you fly me straight back toward them!" A muscle tightens beneath the fur of his neck. "All because you need to deliver some stupid boxes!"

The words settle into my chest. It hurts more than it should. Because he's right. I kept where we were heading a secret from him—or I tried to. Should've known he'd figure it out. He probably reads star charts better than I do.

But I never would have predicted this. That his response to learning we're headed toward his prison would be to start planning a break-in.

"It's a bad idea," I say, trying to calm my voice. "You'd be flying to your death, or worse. And you don't even know…"

He's still glaring at me. "Don't even know what?"

Whether your friend is alive or dead.

This isn't the first time he's mentioned that friend. My view hasn't changed. The company is spending sixty thousand credits to retrieve the Expie sitting in front of me, with a price tag they've broadcasted to every pirate with a pulse. That kind of desperation only comes from losing something irreplaceable.

But I'm not saying that now. Not when we're both a raised voice away from screaming at each other.

I exhale through my nose. "You don't even know how to get into whatever this place is—or out, for that matter. Some random pirate captain threw you a bone the first time. Those bones don't grow on trees."

His muzzle scrunches. "What does that mean? What's a tree?"

"It means you got lucky. Once. Luck like that don't happen twice."

He holds my gaze. "Do you know the best way to win a dogfight against a superior ship?"

"Turn tail and run."

"Wrong." His paw lifts from the armrest and points at me. "You should know, because you've already done it. Be unpredictable. Attack when they don't expect it. Attack what they least expect."

"Let me guess." I drag my palm down one half of my face. "Strolling right back to the Spindle—whatever it is—is something the company won't expect."

"Exactly."

"Flying this ship straight into a black hole is also unexpected. Should we try that as well?"

"False equivalence."

"What?"

"You heard me."

"Should I start listing out the real issues, then? We're flying a crippled hauler with charge-cycle weapons that kill our power, while being hunted by every damn pirate in the sector!" I clench my jaw and force my voice back to a normal volume. "You don't even have a plan, do you?"

Ollie finally blinks. "Do you? What do we do after we make it to Lidfield or whatever? Deliver more boxes? How many more pirates can you blow up before the bone trees stop growing?"

Blow up. His way of avoiding the painful word for what I did. Whether that word is kill or murder, I don't even know myself.

I squeeze my eyes shut. I'm sleep-deprived, in pain, and in a few hours I've got to stare down Solen through a comms screen and pretend I wasn't just fondling the thing he's paying sixty grand to get back. I don't have the energy for this argument. Not now.

Ollie just needs to know this discussion isn't over. "Let's see about making it to Lindenfield in one piece, first. Then we can discuss how best to fuck everything up."

"Fine."

Ollie spins the chair back toward the viewport. The black swallows him. His tail curls around the base of the seat.

Then, barely loud enough to hear: "Asshole."

"Hey!" Heat floods my face. Not anger—the swear just sounds wrong in his voice. "Who taught you that word?"

He glances back over his shoulder. His muzzle curls. "I've been listening to the pirate channel during my shifts." The curl deepens. "They use some funny words."

Of course they do. And he's been absorbing every last syllable like a little furry sponge.

Bad language might be the least of my worries—but it's the only one I can fix. "Say that again," I tell him, "and there won't be any more borrowed fur."

He snaps upright. His tail clamps the chair and he spins halfway back, eyes wide, mouth open. "But—but you swear all the—"

"Captain's orders." I fold my arms. "Do I make myself clear, co-pilot?"

His mouth closes. His claws drum against the armrest as his gaze darts between my face and some invisible middle distance. Maybe he's thinking the same thing I am—that I liquified three pirates and now have the gall to ban swears—but we've both silently agreed not to point it out.

"Fine…"

The sigh that comes out of him is long and strange. It could be the disappointment of someone having his vocabulary restricted—or the relief of someone who's just been promised a next time.

Sleep doesn't come easy. Don't know if it comes at all. Just hours of tossing and shivering, the agony in my leg ratcheting up like the jaws of a bear trap slowly carving through my flesh. Every position is wrong. Every breath shifts the splint a fraction, and the gravel inside my ankle grinds and grinds. Makes me want to saw the whole thing off and just tip out whatever's left in there.

I punch down on the mattress and grit my teeth. Fear of needles be damned—right about now I'd give my left nut for Ollie to jab a syringe into my neck. One in each vertebra if that's what it takes.

I don't know how long I toss and turn. Time doesn't move properly when you're counting the seconds between throbs. At some point, I crack open an eye.

And my body goes limp.

Colours. Colours that have no business being on this ship. Greens and purples and blues, rippling across the dead ceiling like light reflected off water. They move in slow, rolling waves—pooling in the corners, then retreating toward the cockpit before rippling back.

A sudden flash of violet washes over my skin—bright enough to make me flinch. It softens to a deep emerald that creeps along the bunk frame and drips down the wall like wet paint.

I grab the crutch.

The cockpit doorway is a rectangle of shifting colour—now turquoise and magenta. As I pass through, it becomes a white so pure that it leaves an afterimage when it fades.

Ollie's there in the pilot's chair, bare-furred, legs tucked beneath him. He's staring through the viewport. I follow his gaze, and the pain in my ankle forgets to exist.

A nebula. Massive. Pillars of superheated gas filling the portside viewport from edge to edge—a wall of luminous cloud that doesn't end, just thins where colour bleeds into black. Deep within, something detonates—a silent flash that sends tendrils of plasma arcing outward like white veins, branching and splitting, streaking from cloud to cloud in chains that stretch for what must be thousands of kilometres.

"It rolled in about an hour ago," Ollie says. "I can't look away."

Neither can I. A streak of gold races across the console and rolls over Ollie, so vivid I almost forget the colour of his fur.

"Don't worry," he adds. "I know better than to fly into it."

The nebula pulses. Deep blue ripples through the viewport, slow as a tide. I close my eyes and watch the colours dance beneath my eyelids.

Then the thought returns—the one that kept me company through every sleepless hour in that bunk.

"No sign of Solen?"

Ollie's ear-things twitch. He's still staring out the viewport. Mesmerised.

"Ollie."

"Hm?"

"Solen. Any sign?"

"He called an hour ago."

It takes a moment to process. "What… does that mean?"

Ollie's bandaged paw reaches for the console and flicks up the comms panel. There, in the lower corner of the display, a flashing indicator. One inbound request. The timestamp reads sixty-four minutes ago.

"He's kept the request open this whole time," Ollie says.

I stare at the blinking light. Sixty-five minutes now. "And you didn't think to wake me?"

"I don't think you should speak to him." Ollie finally breaks from the viewport and looks back over his shoulder. His eyes find mine, and the colour in them shifts from orange to red. The nebula paints a streak of crimson across his muzzle. "Nothing good will come of it."

"Get off the chair. That isn't for you to decide."

He holds his gaze for one more moment, his eyes dimming as the nebula calms. Then he uncurls his legs, slides off the seat, and retreats to the portside wall without a word.

I swallow the rest of the scolding. Ollie could have killed the call before I ever knew it existed. But he kept Solen on hold, waiting this whole time. For a man busy enough to have an executive assistant, he sure isn't in a hurry.

I lower myself into the pilot's chair and adjust the seat. The leather is warm where Ollie sat. Beyond the viewport, a fork of plasma lightning splits the nebula's interior, briefly turning the clouds white before they flood back to purple.

"Make sure you stay off camera."

Ollie presses himself against the wall. His tail wraps around his ankles. "What are you going to say to him?"

I've spent hours thinking about it. The problem I'm facing: what happened at Kiren has me backed into a corner. I have to assume everything—from the fake customs inspection to the dead man on the factory floor—has found its way back to Solen in a printed memo placed neatly on his desk. They know someone else launched the ship. And they know Chasma Couriers' corporate record shows one employee on the books—me.

Just me.

Pleading innocence won't trick a sly man like Solen. But what sane man would tell a lie that incriminates himself? A lie so damning, so self-destructive, that Solen would have no choice but to believe it's true.

"What I'm about to do," I tell Ollie, "won't feel good."

"Huh?"

"But no matter what I say, you have to stay quiet. Okay?"

Ollie shifts against the wall, fidgeting with his paws. His bandaged wrist turns over and over in his grip. He doesn't look at me. "What are you going to say?"

I can't say it—not to Ollie's face. "Just promise me."

A long pause. The nebula flashes.

"I… I promise." He still doesn't look.

Guess it's now or never. I take a deep breath. And tap accept.

Static crackles across the top monitor. The image assembles itself in stages—a dim office, dark wood, the shape of a man in a tailored suit behind a desk that's barely visible. Swept-back grey hair catches the light from a source I can't see. And that smile. That mechanical, market-tested smile that moves one frame behind the rest of his facial features.

He doesn't seem the least bit surprised I answered, despite waiting on the line for over an hour. As if there was never any doubt.

"Ah, Mr Calyx." The smile settles. "We meet again."

Still not my name. "Solen."

"I must say, you've caused me no short order of worry." He leans forward, hands lacing together on the desk. "When you failed to answer, I feared the worst."

A tendril of green light crawls across the console between us. We stare at each other. The monitor flickers in its lower corner, and for once I'm grateful for the distraction—something to anchor my eyes to when his smile becomes too much.

Solen tilts his head a fraction. "Typically, in a normal conversation, you'd, uh… reply to my concerns with a hint of empathy."

"That so?"

"Quite so." He studies me through the screen. "But I must say, you look most displeased."

"I can't tell whether to be angrier at you or myself."

Solen blinks. He leans back in his chair. Whatever script he had prepared, this isn't the opening line he expected. "How so?"

"You warned me, didn't you? Wasn't a good warning, but you tried."

"Uh… about?"

"I remember the words exactly. 'These experiments are resourceful.' 'Too much hope makes them unpredictable.'" Outside the viewport, a white flash detonates somewhere deep in the nebula, and for a split second Solen's face is washed in brilliance. "I should have taken you seriously."

By the time the flash dims, Solen's smile has died. What's left isn't an expression at all. Just a face.

"Because if I did," I say, "I wouldn't have had to watch a living, breathing creature splatter his brains across my cargo hold."

The softest whimper to my side. I don't dare glance at Ollie. I keep my eyes on Solen with as much lifelessness as I can muster—the flat stare of a hollow man.

Solen's jaw shifts. A canine digs into his lower lip. The silence goes on long enough that I start to hear the starboard engine struggling through the deck.

When he finally responds, it's with a single word:

"When?"

"After your fuckwit stooges at Kiren scared the will to live out of him." I let the anger surface—don't have to fake that part. "Poor thing bawled his eyes out about how you wouldn't let him get away. How it was just a matter of time before they caught him." I look down into my lap. Study my hands. "Thought he'd calm down. Didn't think he'd… he'd…"

Cold blue crawls across the cockpit.

"Proof," Solen says. "Show me proof. Now."

"Gave him a space burial. Threw him off the platform at Harlow." I look up. He's still not smiling. I've never seen his face without it. It's worse. "You're welcome to go look for him there. But I think the bounty's run its course. Call it off."

"I'm afraid whatever… drama befell you at Kiren must have involved a head injury." He raises his hand, and a finger goes up. "You concealed a Company asset." A second finger. "Misled a Company investigator." A third. "Injured a Company employee." And a fourth, his pinky, presented with the faintest flicker of a smile creeping back. "And through your negligence, you destroyed a Company asset. Not really in a position to make demands, are we?"

"That asset told me some interesting things." My turn to smile. "Things that, if the wrong ears heard, could be rather damaging, I think."

Solen scoffs. The smile is back now, the kind you'd give a child who's said something cute but fundamentally stupid. "The testimony of some space courier living below the poverty line, claiming he's got his hands on Company secrets." He waves a hand. "Maybe you'll find some gutter press mouthpiece as brain-damaged as yourself."

"Or maybe that's a risk not worth taking. From the company's perspective. From your perspective." A purple wave rolls across the viewport; I can see it reflected in Solen's monitor, painting the edges of his suit lavender. "I'm not asking for much. Just to put this month from hell behind me and never think of it again."

"And how, exactly, am I supposed to know that you're telling the truth?"

"Really? You think I picked up the call and admitted to hiding him—so I could keep hiding him? What do you want? A photo of his yellow bloodstain on my hull?"

That's something I could provide. Ollie's arm made a bit of a mess.

Solen doesn't respond. He just stares at me—the scowl of someone who's met his match. A small, irritating variable in an otherwise orderly spreadsheet. Knowing the colour of Ollie's blood means he can't dismiss this as a lie. That isn't something you guess.

"Again," I say, "I'm not asking for much. Just for this to go away. For both of us. You won't even need to pay sixty grand for the privilege."

Solen raises his head. He blinks, rapidly. Then, all at once, the cold demeanour evaporates, and that smile crawls back.

"Well… this is most disappointing." He adjusts his cuffs. "I suppose next time—heaven forbid there be a next time—I'll need to be a tad more explicit about the risks these Sawians pose. To others… and themselves."

Outside the viewport, a slow cascade of yellow unfurls through the nebula's upper reaches, like blood seeping through a hoodie.

Risks they pose. As if the only lesson from a person's death is a bullet point buried in a briefing document.

I shift in the chair. "Can I ask you something?"

"Of course."

"How do you live with yourself?"

"Excuse me?"

"To make a living being decide his only way out is a bullet to the brain. What the hell did you do?"

A flash of plasma arcs silently through the nebula. When it fades, Solen's expression has changed. Just slightly. A squint that could almost pass for offence.

"I didn't do anything."

"You—"

"I don't know what that branch of our Company does, and I don't care to know." His smile thins but holds. "My role is asset retrieval."

"That's your excuse? Wilful blindness?"

"Yes, Mr Calyx—that's how a company works. Cogs, gears, silos, whatever tired metaphor you like." He opens his palms as if presenting the most obvious thing in the universe. "Do I look like a biogeneticist to you?"

He looks like an overdressed used-ship salesman. But I'd rather get one more thing out of him before I start laying on the insults.

"One last question."

"Anything to delay all the paperwork you've forced upon me." He doesn't sound too displeased about that.

"Why did you need him back?"

"Why—"

"Shut up and let me finish." I grip the armrest. This call has gone on way too long, but I need to ask this—I need to put Ollie's terrible plan to bed. "You… the company… they already have another Expie that's the same as Ollie, don't they?"

The nebula shifts. Deep violet bleeds through the viewport and settles on my skin, the same colour as my ankle. In the harsh light, Solen's face takes on an almost alien quality. The hollows of his cheeks darken, and the line of his jaw sharpens.

"Interesting." He draws the word out, almost playfully. "It certainly shared a lot, didn't it? Must have trusted you."

"Suppose for you, being trusted isn't a familiar feeling."

"Hah." A dry, clipped non-laugh. "You have a curious way of trying to extract confidential information. Never thought insulting the counterparty would be so productive, but here we are."

"If it's so productive, where's my answer?"

Solen leans back. The leather of his chair creaks through the speakers. He steeples his fingers and presses them to his lips, and for a long moment he just watches me through the screen.

"How much do you know about horse racing, Mr Calyx?"

I nearly laugh. How much does a man who hasn't been planetside in over a decade know about horse racing? "I know what a horse looks like."

"It's a fascinating sport. Truly. A storied history spanning centuries."

"If you say so."

"Here's a mind-bender for you." He unlaces his fingers and holds up one hand. "Do you know how many stallions the entire Thoroughbred breed descends from?"

"I have a feeling you're going to tell me."

"Three. Centuries of champions, and every one of them runs back to three horses." The smile reaches his eyes. "One stallion buys you a single generation. But two buys you a breed."

The nebula seems to calm, shimmering silently beyond the glass. Solen waits, studying me for a reaction.

"Okay?" I scratch my cheek. "So, three stallions, but they only needed two?"

"Indeed."

Two horses. Two Expies.

No. Don't overthink it. He's just messing with you. Getting under your skin.

I clear my throat and try to laugh. It sounds more like a cough. "Were you a jockey in your past life?"

"I used to be a detective, in fact. Worked planetside."

"Really?" I glance at his charcoal suit and swept-back hair. "I thought you were born wearing that suit."

"Detectives wear suits."

Do they? I've never met one.

"It might sound surprising," he continues, "but there are a lot of transferrable skills between the two roles."

"Suppose you spent a lot of time tracking things down as a detective."

"Not as much as you'd expect." He shifts in his chair, and his tone loosens. The smooth corporate gives way to something conversational, like he's telling a story at a bar. "Tracking is so much easier planetside. Everyone carries a personal device, pinging a dozen cellular towers every few seconds. Finding someone was as simple as calling them, keeping them on the line with some inane topic, and then triangulating their position based on which towers facilitated the call."

Lightning crackles through the nebula, splitting into a dozen branches. The cockpit turns white. My fingers tighten around the armrest.

"Huh. That easy?"

"That easy." He nods. "Mind you, finding someone is always easier on a planet. Space, less so."

He lets that settle.

"That was, until Princeton Communications got their long-range relay network up and running. They make a killing off these calls, you know."

Something moves in the corner of my vision. Ollie's paw is braced against the console housing. His claws have sunk into the metal.

"It works on the same principle, really. Thousands of tiny beacons scattered through space, all at once trying to ping a ship drifting who-knows-where, until enough beacons locate it and establish a stable enough connection to stream a call."

The horrible feeling starts in my gut. Spreads. My ankle fires a lance of pain up my shin as every bone in my body tenses at once.

A blur to my left.

Ollie launches himself onto the console, paws slamming down on the interface. His claw drives the end-call button through the housing. Solen's features dissolve into static, breaking apart pixel by pixel, until his smile vanishes last.

The monitor goes dark.

Ollie stares at the dead screen. His jaw hangs limp. His eyes are wide, the orange blown out by the nebula light washing over his fur. His entire body is trembling.

No. No. We're both overthinking it—we must be. "He's… he's bluffing," I say.

Ollie doesn't say anything. His breathing is shallow and fast. Slowly, his paws swipe the screen. He navigates back to the comms panel. To the list of saved frequencies. His claw hovers, then taps the one without a name—the spread-spectrum connection to the pirate channel.

The cockpit fills with static. The nebula writhes outside the viewport. Emerald light floods the glass, turning the monitor green.

We wait.

Seconds stretch into minutes. The static drones on. My ankle throbs in time with my heartbeat—both too fast to be healthy.

I start to breathe. Start to think maybe—

"Good afternoon."

Posh. Polite. A generic greeting. Nothing but dread. One of Solen's ilk. Not him. He doesn't even care enough to tie the noose himself.

"Offer increased to seventy-five thousand credits for the retrieval of stolen proprietary technology. Callsign: Calyx. Kestrel-class, model ten."

A pause. Another flash. The cockpit strobes white.

"Precise location: Sector DS Seven-Eight-Delta, X-axis one-nine-eight-point-four, Y-axis negative thirty-four-point-two, Z-axis ninety-two-point-five. Please arrive promptly. Technology must be unharmed. Pilot is expendable."

The transmission ends. The static lasts only a second before—

"Let's get this cunt!"

"You pricks piss off—he's mine."

As the speakers crackle with half-a-dozen overlapping voices, Ollie's paw slides off the console. He turns to look at me. The nebula paints him in shifting colour. Violet. Blue. Then a deep, terrible red.
